### Security: connections that send no request, or take none of their response, no longer hold a place under the connection limit

`HTTP_HEADER_READ_TIMEOUT_SECS` applied only once a connection was known to be HTTP/1. A client
that connected and sent nothing, sent part of the HTTP/2 connection preface, or opened an HTTP/2
connection without a request was never disconnected, and each such connection kept its place under
the open connection limit. A client could also keep its place by asking for a response and never
taking it: an HTTP/1 client that stops reading the socket, or an HTTP/2 client that keeps its
flow-control window shut. A few client networks could take every place without signing in, and
the server then refused every other connection ([GH#665], [GH#682]).

A connection with no request in progress for `HTTP_HEADER_READ_TIMEOUT_SECS` (default 10 s),
counted from when it was accepted or from when its last request started or ended, is now closed
whatever its protocol; an HTTP/2 client is sent a `GOAWAY` first. HTTP/2 clients reconnect when
they need to, as HTTP/1 clients already did after the same idle time.

A connection whose client takes none of a response for the new `HTTP_SEND_TIMEOUT_SECS` (optional,
default 60 s, 1 to 3600) is dropped. Each response is tracked on its own, so on an HTTP/2
connection one stream taking data does not keep alive another the client holds shut. Progress is
counted in small steps whatever the size of the response: every 16 KiB taken on HTTP/2, and on
HTTP/1 (Linux) about every 100 to 200 KiB, depending on how much the kernel queues unsent. A client
that reads slowly but steadily is not cut. A response that is slow to come from the server is not
limited. No API or schema change.

A client that keeps taking a response slowly can still hold its connection for as long as the
response lasts; a minimum transfer rate is not enforced. With the default 60 s that is about 16 KiB
per minute on HTTP/2, or about 2 to 3 KiB/s on HTTP/1, bounded by the per-client connection limit.
Outside Linux, the server only notices progress once the
socket's send buffer has drained by about a third, so a client reading well under 1 Mbit/s with a
large send buffer can be cut there; raise `HTTP_SEND_TIMEOUT_SECS` if that matters.
